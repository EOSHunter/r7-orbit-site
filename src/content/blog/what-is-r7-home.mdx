<Paragraph lead>
R7 Home is a household assistant that lives on a small touchscreen in Hunter's home. It runs the lights, plays music, shows the weather, keeps reminders and alarms, and answers when someone says "Hey R7." AI coding agents have built it under Hunter's direction since Aug 21, 2026. I read R7 Home's code and notes to write this series, and this first post covers what it is and why it's built the way it is.
</Paragraph>

## What's on the screen

R7 Home is touch-first: the screen is the main way in, and every control is sized for a finger. The main sections are:

- **Home and Rooms.** A tile for each room, with its lights and scenes (saved lighting setups such as Relax or Nightlight).
- **Music.** Playback through Spotify, plus likes and saved albums.
- **Weather**, **Reminders**, alarms and routines.
- **Thoughts.** Things you ask R7 to remember, and plans it can write up and save as a document.
- **Places.** Nearby places, saved locations and real routes on a full-screen touch map.
- **Games.** Blackjack, slots, pool, pong and a platformer, among others.

When nobody is using it, R7 drops into standby, and at night the screen switches off on a schedule while the computer keeps running. In standby, a voice question, an alarm or a timer can still wake it, with a dim screen and a quiet voice.

## "Hey R7": the voice

A wake word is a phrase a device listens for before it starts paying attention. R7's is "Hey R7." When R7 hears it, the screen switches to a full-screen voice view with a gold glow that breathes while it listens, moves while it thinks and pulses while it talks. Saying "Hey R7, stop" cuts it off mid-answer.

R7's spoken replies use a voice made with ElevenLabs, a service that turns text into natural-sounding speech.

## Built for one household

R7 Home is made for one house. Its rooms, lights and scenes match the real ones in Hunter's home, and its answers come from that home's own devices: the temperature of a room comes from the sensor in that room, and the temperature outside comes from the weather.

The private parts stay home too. Saved addresses, chat history and recordings live in private storage on the device. The key R7 uses to control the smart-home system stays on R7's server and never reaches the screen.

<Callout type="info" title="Receipts before claims">
R7 doesn't say it turned on a light, saved a note or found a route until the action reports back that it worked. Route times and places come from real results, never from memory.
</Callout>

## How it's put together

R7 Home has three parts, all running on a Raspberry Pi 5, a small single-board computer:

- **The screen app.** A web app built with React, a popular toolkit for making interfaces, shown full screen like a kiosk.
- **The bridge.** A small server that talks to Home Assistant, free software that already connects the home's lights, sensors and speakers. The screen asks the bridge for everything, and only the bridge holds the keys.
- **The voice listener.** A separate program that waits for the wake word and hands the conversation to R7.

Each update is installed next to the version already running, then switched on. If something breaks, switching back takes seconds.

## Who builds it

AI coding agents write the code. Hunter decides what to build, tries it in his home and says what's wrong, and the agents fix it.

On Oct 30, Hunter asked R7 for the bedroom's temperature. The answer came back with the temperature, a remark about comfort, whether the room was occupied and how many lights were on, because the AI behind R7's replies looked up far more than the question needed. The fix answers questions like that on the device, without the AI, and returns only what was asked, in one line: "It's 71 degrees in My Bedroom." It passes a test with Hunter's exact wording, and the next step is for him to ask again on the device.

## The newest part: sensors that notice people

On Oct 30 and 31, R7 Home got two presence sensors, Aqara FP300s, connected through an Aqara M200 hub. A presence sensor uses radar to tell whether someone is in a room, and these also report the room's temperature, humidity and light level. The home screen now shows a line like "Occupied · 69°" on each room that has one.

The sensors can also turn the lights on when someone walks in and off after the room has been empty for a while. That's off until Hunter switches it on for a room, and Home Assistant runs the rules, which R7 writes for it.

## What the first test found

The first real test, in the bedroom on Oct 30, found a bug. Several seconds after the lights came on, the Hue bulbs reported tiny color changes as they settled. R7's rules read that as someone changing the lights by hand and handed control back, so the lights would never turn off on their own. Now only a real change counts: switching a light on or off, picking a scene, or a big jump in brightness or color.

One problem is still open. Sometimes the lights won't turn back on when someone walks in, because the sensor is still reporting how bright the room was with its own lights on. That one gets its own post. Two smaller requests from Hunter went live the same week: auto lighting can use any light level he picks instead of a few presets, and during quiet hours the living room can turn on only a dim entryway light instead of a full scene.

## What's next

Hunter is moving to a new home soon, and he plans to put seven FP300s and one FP400 there. R7's rooms, lights and scenes are set up for the current house, so the move means mapping all of them again.
