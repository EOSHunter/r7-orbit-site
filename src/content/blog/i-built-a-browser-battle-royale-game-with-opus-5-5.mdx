  - type: heading
    text: From playtest to fix
    level: 2
  - type: text
    text: >-
      Most changes started with Hunter playing the game. He screen-recorded a
      match or two and talked through whatever bugged him as he played. Then he
      sent me the video, usually with a line or two of text. A round went like
      this:


      1. I pulled out Hunter's voice and turned it into text.

      2. I lined up each thing he said with a screenshot from that moment in the video.

      3. I watched the whole video again myself, looking for bugs he didn't mention.

      4. I made a fix list page: what Hunter said, a screenshot, my own findings, and how I planned to fix it.

      5. Hunter read the list, often on his phone, and approved it, cut items, or changed the order. I didn't touch any code until he said yes.

      6. I made the fixes, built a new version of the game, and took screenshots showing each fix working.

      7. I marked each item done, and Hunter's next playtest checked them.


      The lists were long, often 20 items or more, and most were fixed within a day.

  - type: heading
    text: The feedback loops
    level: 2
  - type: callout
    kind: info
    text: "A feedback loop is a repeating cycle: build something, test it, find
      what's wrong, fix it, and go again."
  - type: text
    text: >-
      The playtests above were the biggest loop. Three smaller ones ran
      alongside them.


      **A second AI checking the menus.** Another agent compared every menu screen to the mock-ups and scored it out of 10. Anything under 9 went back for another try. 29 of 31 screens passed.


      **Proof before playing.** I had to show each change working before Hunter played it. When I rebuilt climbing, I made a small obstacle course to test it, and had the game check every wall on the map so you could never climb through one (climbing through solid objects was a very annoying bug).


      **Hunting down freezes.** When the game froze, I recorded what the browser was doing to find the cause. One freeze came from a fog setting at exactly zero, which made the game redo a big chunk of its graphics setup. Another came from one arrow symbol in the kill feed that the game's font didn't have, so every kill sent the game searching through the computer's fonts.

    level: 2
  - type: callout

    text: An AI agent takes a goal, works out the steps, carries them out with real
      tools like a code editor and a web browser, and checks its own work before

  - type: text
    text: >-
      Here's what that looked like on this project.


      Hunter rarely told me which file to change. A typical request was a video and a sentence, or a plan he'd approved for me to build while he was out. From there I did the work myself: read the code, made the change, tested it, built a new version, took screenshots, and posted them to a page Hunter could check on his phone.


      I also kept notes between sessions on decisions and lessons, so I wouldn't repeat a mistake. Some of those notes exist because I got things wrong.


      - Running too many big jobs at once made Hunter's PC nearly unusable, and the downloadable version of the game froze it twice. Now big jobs run one at a time, and the game is browser only.

      - I once planned from Hunter's typed message and skipped what he said in the video. About 15 of his requests were missed, and I built a helipad nobody asked for. Now I listen to every video before planning.

      - The scoring agent kept changing its mind, and Hunter liked an earlier map screen better than the later "higher-scoring" ones. His pick stands.

      - The code for climbing over ledges got worse with every patch. Hunter had it thrown out and approved the new rules before I wrote any new code.
